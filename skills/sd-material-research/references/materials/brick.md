# Fired-clay brick masonry (brick walls): reference sheet

> **Scope:** fired-clay facing brick laid in mortar, seen face-on as a wall, from new to heavily weathered. Covers stretcher (running), common (American), Flemish, English, English cross and stack bonds; joint profiles from flush to tuck pointing; UK, US, EU (DE, NL) and AU unit sizes; extruded wirecut, soft-mud stock, handmade and dry-pressed bricks; lime and cement mortars, including repointing. **Not covered:** glazed brick and tile faces, brick paving (horizontal traffic wear), refractory and firebrick, engineering-only uses (sewers, below ground), painted or rendered brick, structural cracking. Missing bricks get one line in section 5.
> **Researched:** 2026-10-02. **Confidence:** sizes, tolerances, bond and joint rules, chip limits and decay mechanisms are well sourced (standards, BIA, BDA, NPS, SPAB, ICOMOS, peer-reviewed papers). Arris-rounding radii, spall counts per m², real-wall per-brick colour spread, wet darkening of brick, mortar albedo and every roughness value are estimates or values from the user's brick project.

Every number carries a source tag `[n]` (see Sources) or `(est.)` with a one-line reason. `[B]` marks a value from the user's yellow London-stock brick project (`brick_materials_tools/`, review round 3; raked joints 5.8-7.4 mm, 1.8 m tile). Some [B] values are measurements of that material and some are tuning targets its reviewers set; each says which. Neither is a measurement of real walls, so [B] values never stand alone as physics. Notes marked *from the brick project* are lessons from it about appearance, not about that graph.

## 0. Physical summary

Brick is clay formed into units (extruded and wire-cut, soft-mud moulded, handmade or dry-pressed), dried, then fired until the particles partly fuse (vitrification range 871-1316 °C [19]). Units are laid in courses on mortar beds with mortar in the perpends. The bond and the joint widths are fixed by the bricklayer and do not change afterwards. Weathering then works on two materials that sit **side by side** in the face: brick and mortar. Procedural versions most often get these three facts wrong:

1. **Erosion of a brick removes brick and reveals brick.** Rounded arrises, spalled faces, chips and lime pops expose the brick body, which can differ in colour from the fired surface [20][21][25]. Mortar sits beside the brick face, not beneath it, so no amount of brick loss exposes more mortar, and the joint footprint keeps its width. Worn edges only make a joint *look* wider: NPS warns that pointing out over worn, rounded edges gives "a joint which is visually wider than the actual joint" [17]. How deep a break may go next to the joint depends on the joint profile. In a raked or recessed joint the damage stays above the recessed mortar (the user's rule [B], section 5 I5). In a flush, struck or concave joint a deep spall can drop below the mortar face, and the mortar is left standing round the hollow as a ledge [31]: its side shows, but the joint still keeps its width in plan.
2. **Which material erodes first depends on the mortar's strength and permeability relative to the brick, not on the binder's name.** Ideally the mortar face and the brick face erode at about the same rate [15], which is a third state in its own right. When the mortar is the weaker, more permeable part (lime on any brick, and usually cement-lime on hard modern brick), the joint recesses first and the arrises are then exposed [15][17]. When the mortar is stronger and denser than the brick (cement repointing on soft brick, or strong 19th-century and hydraulic mixes [15]), moisture has to leave through the brick, its faces flake, and the mortar is "left standing proud of decayed bricks" [16][15]. In every case the joint stays where the layout put it.
3. **Weathering follows water and shelter, not uniform noise.** Wind-driven rain wets top corners and edges most [36]. Rain-washed areas stay clean while sheltered areas collect soiling and black crust [28][35]. Splash, rising damp and salts concentrate decay in the lowest 0.15-1 m [38][39][30], and sills and copings shed runoff onto the wall below [38][11]. Colour varies per unit, not per pixel, because each brick has its own firing history [19][21].

## 1. Construction and layout

### 1.1 Bonds
- **Stretcher (running) bond:** all stretchers, half-lapped. It is the bond of cavity walls and veneers, which are tied with metal ties instead of headers [1].
- **Common (American) bond:** running bond plus a course of full headers at every 5th, 6th or 7th course [1]. The header course starts each way from the corner with a three-quarter brick. A Flemish header course is a common variant [1].
- **Flemish bond:** every course alternates header and stretcher, and the headers of alternate courses are centred over the stretchers between them [1]. In veneers the headers are half bricks ("clipped" or "snap" headers) [1]. Garden-wall variants put 2 to 5 stretchers between headers [1]. In 18th-century American colonial work the headers were often glazed, because they sat nearest the heat in the kiln: blue-grey in Virginia and Maryland, black in Philadelphia [26].
- **English bond:** alternate courses of headers and stretchers. Headers are centred on stretchers, and the perpends between stretchers align vertically in every stretcher course [1].
- **English cross (Dutch) bond:** as English bond, but the stretcher perpends of alternate stretcher courses fall on the centres of the stretchers above and below [1].
- **Stack bond:** no lap, all perpends aligned. It is a pattern bond only, needs ties or bed-joint reinforcement, and requires pre-matched, accurate units to keep the head joints aligned [1].
- Structurally bonded walls need at least 4% of the face as headers, with at most 24 in (610 mm) between adjacent headers [1]. EC6 requires units to overlap on alternate courses by at least 0.4 x unit height or 40 mm, whichever is greater [9].

### 1.2 Layout rules real bricklayers follow
- Perpends line up vertically every other course (running, English, Flemish) or every fourth course (English cross) [1]. Wander is bounded by the unit size range, because the perpends absorb length differences; with T2/R2 units those differences "will hardly be visible" [4][3].
- Mortar "compensates for dimensional variations in the units" [1]. Masons also adjust bed-joint thickness and course count to reach fixed heights, and slight deviations are not obvious in the finished wall [2].
- Closers (three-quarter bats, or a quarter "queen closer" set one header in from the corner) occur only at corners, reveals and openings [1]. A field tile has none.
- Frogged bricks are laid frog up [11]. Cores and frogs are in the bed faces and never show on a stretcher face.
- Bricks are drawn from at least three packs, vertically or diagonally. Without blending, batch differences cluster into patches or bands, and multi-coloured bricks group by colour [12].

### 1.3 Unit types: how forming and firing show on the face
- **Extruded wirecut** (about 90% of US output [19]): clay is forced through a die and the column is wire-cut into bricks [24]. The die skin is smooth, but most facing bricks have it removed and the face cut, scratched, rolled, brushed or sanded as the column leaves the die [19]. Arrises are crisp and tolerances tighter than stock or handmade [3]. Cores run bed to bed and stay at least 19 mm from every edge [20]. Drag and roller marks follow the extrusion direction (est.: inferred from core orientation; check reference photos for the brick being matched).
- **Soft-mud (stock), machine or hand moulded:** clay with 20-30% water is formed in moulds lubricated with sand (sand-struck, sand-faced) or water (water-struck) [19][24]. Faces carry a fired-on sand coat, folds and soft arrises.
- **Handmade:** a sanded clot thrown into a sanded mould leaves "creases and valleys" on the face and folds on the edges [23] (the creases are sometimes called smiles). Handmade and stock bricks have looser size tolerances than extruded or wirecut ones [3].
- **Dry-pressed:** at most 10% water, pressed at 3.4-10.3 MPa [19]. Smooth, sharp and regular.
- **Surface treatments:** engobes (fired clay slips), rolled-on sands, and tumbling for an antique, rounded edge [19].
- **Firing colour:** iron-bearing clay fires red in an oxidising atmosphere and darkens toward black in a reducing one, which is called flashing [19]. With the same clay and works, hotter firing gives darker colour, lower absorption and higher strength [19]. It also shrinks more (firing shrinkage 2.5-4%), so the dark units of a range run slightly smaller than the light ones [19].
- **Clamp firing:** bricks nearest the heat over-fire, vitrify and can slump (clinker); under-fired ones can be too soft for external use [21]. London stocks get their yellow from chalk; "spanish" (household ash and cinders) mixed in burned as fuel [22].
- **Within one brick:** colour varies across the face with air movement in the kiln or clamp and the way the bricks were stacked [21]. These setting marks, sometimes called kiss marks, are patches or diagonal bands. The core can differ from the surface: a brick that is red on the surface "may have a core of yellow or deep grey" [21]. Chips show more on bricks whose surface colour differs from the body [20].
- **Inclusions:** calcium-carbonate particles near the face turn to quicklime in firing and later expand, popping a shallow crater that "reveal[s] the body colour of fired clay". The reaction is non-progressive [25].

### 1.4 Per-brick colour variation
- ASTM sets no colour tolerances; the range is defined by a sample panel [20]. Colour and size vary between batches [12].
- Within one clay and one works, darker correlates with hotter firing, lower absorption, higher strength and slightly smaller size; across different clays there is no such relation [19]. Flashing is a reducing atmosphere, not by itself a hotter firing [19]. So within one range the pale, under-fired bricks are the ones that weather and spall first (est.: follows from [19][21]). Do not co-locate spalls with flashed ends.
- Per-brick face-luma CV measured on the brick project: 4.1% classic, 4.7% weathered, 6.7% rustic [B]. Its reviewers set a tiling target of every brick within 0.86-1.14 x the mean face luma (measured 0.92-1.12, 0.89-1.13, 0.87-1.17) [B]. That band is a limit for tiling, not for real walls: multi-blends, flared headers and unblended walls fall outside it. Outliers repeat at the tile period and print a lattice (*from the brick project*).
- Unblended packs give bands about 0.5-1 m tall (est.: a pack of roughly 400-500 bricks at 59 bricks/m² covers 7-8 m²). Use this only as a deliberate "poor workmanship" variant.

### 1.5 What the layout fixes
- Brick outlines, joint centrelines and joint footprints, bond pattern, course gauge, perpend alignment, and per-brick identity (colour class, flash end, texture direction, cores, frog).
- **No aging process changes them.** Only deliberate human work can change the *visible* mortar: pointing feathered over worn edges [17][16][18], ribbon pointing that stands proud of the face [15][16], or power-saw and disc raking that breaks brick edges and overcuts head joints [17][15]. These are opt-in variants with their own masks.

Depths are below the as-built face of each component. The two components sit side by side; each has its own stack.

**Original surface envelope.** The as-built surface is each brick face (with its own small tilt and undulation, at most 1.6-2.4 mm of distortion on faces up to 203 mm for new facing brick [20]) plus the as-built joint profile, including a tuck ribbon that stands proud. Removal processes (erosion, spalling, chips, lime pops, mortar recession, disc damage) only go below it. Only deposits (soiling, crusts, salts, lime run, biological growth) and later human additions (repointing that is proud or feathered, ribbon pointing) may sit above it, and each needs its own mask.

## 4. Processes: aging, wear, damage, deposition

Preset stages used below: **N** new (0-5 years), **W** weathered (30-80 years), **H** heavily weathered or neglected (100+ years). The ages are est.: they are typical repointing and soiling cycles, not measured rates.

### 4.1 Arris wear and rounding
- **Mechanism:** edges dry, wet and freeze from two sides, so grain loss, frost and salts attack them first. ICOMOS calls this rounding: "preferential erosion of originally angular stone edges leading to a distinctly rounded profile" [28]. Once the mortar face recedes, the arrises become "more vulnerable to erosion" [15].
- **Acts on:** brick B1, then B2. **Removes:** brick. **Reveals:** the brick body of the same unit.
- **Never:** widens the mortar footprint or turns the worn band mortar-coloured; rounds the mortar into the brick; goes beyond the as-made radius on N bricks.
- **Where:** all four arrises, strongest where lime joints have receded [15], on wall corners and top and side edges (most wind-driven rain [36]), in the damp base zone, where rounded brick corners are typical [30], and on soft, under-fired bricks [21]. Hard, dark, flashed units resist it [19].
- **Shape and scale:** quarter-round to blunt-chamfer profile. Radius: N wirecut ≤1 mm, stock 1-3 mm; W 2-4 mm; H 5-10 mm (est. throughout: no measured source). The edge line is irregular at grain scale with small notches, and brick corners round more than straight edges (est.: three exposed sides).
- **Progression:** N as made; W patchy rounding on exposed bricks; H corners rounded and edges ragged, usually together with 4.8 or 4.5.
- **Interactions:** follows mortar recession (lime) [15]; is fastest beside hard cement pointing [16]. Repointing flush over a worn arris makes the joint "visually wider than the actual joint" [17].
- **Signature per map:** height: a rounded ramp inside the brick footprint. The opening between the brick faces really does widen, by about twice the arris drop, so a joint width measured on height grows with wear while mask_mortar does not. Normal: a wider tilted band, so the joint reads wider in raking light. Albedo: brick body colour, often dirtier in the hollow (est.). Roughness: +0.02-0.05 [B]. AO: a deeper band beside the joint.
- **Severity scale:** none published for brick; ICOMOS terms only [28].
- **Sources:** [15][16][17][19][21][28][30][36].

### 4.2 Face erosion, powdering and fire-skin loss
- **Mechanism:** rain, wind-borne grit, frost and salts slowly remove grains [15][28]. Weak, porous bricks show "surface powdering progressing into the material" [29]. Frost spalling flakes off the fire skin [27]. Abrasive cleaning that removes it leaves bricks "pitted and more prone to decay" [27]; fire-skin loss by weathering is assumed to do the same (est.).
- **Acts on:** B1, then B2. **Removes:** brick. **Reveals:** the body: rougher, more porous, sand coat and flash gone, inclusions exposed.
- **Never:** raises the surface; increases mortar area; erodes neighbouring bricks at one shared rate.
- **Where:** the wettest faces [36], the base zone [30], soft under-fired bricks [21]. Rates differ per brick, so neighbours end up at different depths (differential erosion [28]).
- **Shape and scale:** 0.1-2 mm of general face loss over a century on an upper wall (est.: well below the worst-case basal rate of 0.18-0.33 mm/yr [30]). Up to 42-55 mm in the basal zone [30]. The surface roughens and pits [28].
- **Progression:** N fire skin and sand coat intact. W sand coat thinned on exposed faces, light pitting. H some bricks recessed several mm behind their neighbours and the base zone hollowed.
- **Signature per map:** height lowered per brick, with fine pitting. Albedo shifts from the surface colour toward the body colour (often lighter, less flashed; est.). Roughness rises. Normal gains fine noise.
- **Severity scale:** none standard.
- **Sources:** [15][21][27][28][29][30][36].

### 4.3 Frost spalling (flaking, scaling, chunk cracking)
- **Mechanism:** water in saturated pores freezes and expands, and the face detaches roughly parallel to the surface [27][31]. Under recurring freeze-thaw "brick surfaces may flake or spall, while the mortar joints may crumble" [46]. Field bricks show three forms: powdering in weak bricks; flaking in layered bricks, where stacked flakes delaminate; and cracking in strong bricks, where chunks of "a few to over a dozen mm" wedge out [29].
- **Acts on:** B1 and B2. **Removes:** brick. **Reveals:** the brick body: a floor roughly parallel to the face, with stepped walls.
- **Never:** enters the joint footprint or lowers the mortar; goes deeper than the unit (in practice ≤25 mm, est.); removes the same amount from every brick. It *may* go below the face of a flush or shallow joint: once the brick face recedes, "the surrounding mortar can remain rigid and protrude around the edges of the hollowed brick" [31]. The joint keeps its width; only the mortar's side face shows. In raked joints the user's rule keeps damage above the recessed mortar (I5).
- **Where:** bricks kept wet: parapets, copings, chimneys, freestanding and retaining walls, below sills, near leaking pipes, and the base [15][11][27][31]. Also soft under-fired bricks [21], and bricks behind impermeable pointing [16]. Susceptibility is per brick, so one brick spalls while its neighbour stays intact (est., from [19][21]).
- **Shape and scale:** scale thickness from mm to cm [28]. On soft bricks the centre often crumbles first while the joint holds the edges, leaving a hollowed bowl that spreads outward [31]; on hard bricks chunks wedge out, often at edges [29]. Outlines are irregular and stepped where flake stacks broke. Depth is 2-12 mm typical and 12-25 mm severe (est.: from [28][29]). One spall covers a corner patch up to the whole face of one brick (est.).
- **Progression:** N none. W partial spalls on a few % of bricks; the brick project used 4.3% of brick area, with at most 28% of any one brick [B]. H 10-30% of bricks in exposed zones, some whole faces lost (est.).
- **Interactions:** worsened by salts (4.4) and hard pointing (4.5). Old floors re-weather and re-soil.
- **Signature per map:** height: a 2-12 mm step with 45-60° walls, which read right in [B], and a rough floor. Albedo: the brick's own hue, a little lighter when fresh, never a cream decal. The brick project's targets were fresh floors 1.04-1.15 x the local face luma with |Δb*| ≤ 1.5 and old floors ≤1.06 x; it measured 1.08-1.10 per brick [B]. Roughness: +0.03-0.05 [B]. AO: in the step only. Fresh breaks are never ringed by dark dirt (*from the brick project*).
- **Severity scale:** BS EN 771-1 freeze/thaw durability categories; F2/S2 bricks are specified for severe exposure [11].
- **Sources:** [11][15][16][19][21][27][28][29][31][B].

### 4.4 Salt crystallisation, subflorescence and basal decay
- **Mechanism:** salts carried by moving water crystallise where it evaporates. Crystals inside the pores (subflorescence) push the surface off [28][27]; on the surface they form efflorescence (4.10) [32]. Salts come from ground water, cement-based mortar, trim and sea spray [32][33][11]. With rising damp they concentrate in a band at the top of the wet zone [38].
- **Acts on:** brick B1/B2 and the mortar. **Removes:** material. **Reveals:** the body. Salt crystals show on the newly exposed surface once a scale falls [28].
- **Never:** changes joint footprints; puts salt above the envelope without a deposit mask.
- **Where:** the base zone 0.25-0.8 m [30] up to the tide mark at about 1 m [38]; below sills, copings and leaks [33][15]; coastal sites with salt spray [11]; next to cement-rich materials [33].
- **Shape and scale:** powdering, flaking and layered scaling. Basal recession reaches 42-55 mm, with rounded brick corners [30]. Loss is banded, deepest low down, with a ragged upper edge (est.).
- **Progression:** N none, or a short-lived new-building bloom [33]. W a white line plus light powdering on the lowest courses. H basal bricks hollowed 10-50 mm and joints eroded [30].
- **Signature per map:** height: banded loss. Albedo: a white salt line at the top of the zone and darker damp below it ("moist area" [28]). Roughness rises.
- **Severity scale:** BS EN 771-1 soluble-salt categories (S2 for severe exposure [11]).
- **Sources:** [11][15][27][28][30][32][33][38].

### 4.5 Brick decay beside hard cement pointing
- **Mechanism:** if the mortar is stronger and less permeable than the brick (cement repointing, but also strong 19th-century lime mixes with iron filings, brick dust or coal ash, and selenitic cement [15]), evaporation "can only take place through the external face of the brick". Those faces then flake by frost or salts, "resulting in a wall in which the mortar face stands proud" [15]. A harder mortar relieves stress "through the masonry units", which crack and spall [17]. The bricks erode "in preference as they become the most permeable part of the wall" [16].
- **Acts on:** the brick arris band and face. **Removes:** brick. **Reveals:** brick body.
- **Never:** lowers or widens the cement, which stays at its pointed level; it can only crack and "drop out in short sections" [16]. Never exposes mortar beneath a brick.
- **Where:** soft historic bricks repointed in cement; strongest at the arrises and in a band 5-20 mm beside each joint (est.), and in the wettest zones [36].
- **Shape and scale:** each brick becomes a shallow dish inside a raised grid of mortar. Brick loss beside the joint is 2-5 mm (W) to 5-15 mm (H), so the mortar stands proud by the same amount (est.: no measured source). Edges ragged and flaky.
- **Progression:** fresh cement repoint: flush or feathered onto the arrises [16]. A rich cement mix "shrinks on setting, producing cracks in the pointing" [15]: dark hairlines along the brick/mortar interface, where water enters. W: bricks dish 2-5 mm. H: proud mortar fins with cracked and missing sections.
- **Signature per map:** height: mortar above the local brick (I5, mortar_harder). Albedo: "very hard, smooth and dull grey" mortar [16] beside dished, body-coloured brick. Roughness: cement smoother than lime (est. 0.75-0.85).
- **Sources:** [15][16][17][36].

### 4.6 Impact and handling chips
- **Mechanism:** knocks during "packaging, shipping or on the job site" [20], then in service (est.: vehicles, ladders, bikes). Chipping is the "breaking off of pieces, called chips, from the edges" [28].
- **Acts on:** the arris and corners of one brick. **Removes:** brick. **Reveals:** the body. Chips "are more noticeable on brick that have a surface color different from the body" [20].
- **Never:** spans two bricks or mirrors across a joint (*from the brick project*); turns mortar-coloured; hits every brick.
- **Where:** corners more than straight edges (ASTM allows larger corner chips [20]); external wall corners and reveals; the lowest 0-1.5 m for in-service impacts (est.: impact height).
- **Shape and scale:** new FBX bricks: edge chips ≤3.2 mm and corner chips ≤6.4 mm. FBS rough bricks: ≤7.9 mm and ≤12.7 mm, with up to 15% of bricks reaching 11.1 and 19.1 mm. Total chip length is ≤10% of the face perimeter [20]. The profile is a conchoidal scallop, deepest at the arris and tapering into the face; the brick project's review targets were end/mid depth ≤0.5 and at least twice as long along the edge as deep [B]. In-service impacts are 10-50 mm (est.).
- **Progression:** N the ASTM class distribution. W plus in-service chips on 1-3% of bricks (est.). H more corners lost.
- **Signature per map:** height: a faceted wedge at the arris. Albedo: body colour, lighter when fresh. Roughness: glassy fracture on vitrified bricks, granular on soft ones (est.).
- **Severity scale:** ASTM C216 FBX / FBS / FBA chippage limits [20].
- **Sources:** [20][28][B].

### 4.7 Lime pops (lime blowing)
- **Mechanism:** calcium carbonate particles near the face become quicklime in the kiln. In later wetting they hydrate and expand, causing "a shallow disruption" that reveals "the body colour of fired clay". Once it has happened, the particle is inert and the defect does not progress [25]. ICOMOS calls the general form bursting: an "irregularly-sided crater" from internal pressure [28].
- **Acts on:** B1. **Removes:** a small cone of brick. **Reveals:** the body, with a white lime residue at the bottom that weathers off [25].
- **Never:** grows over time [25]; hits every brick.
- **Where:** randomly per brick, in bricks from calcareous clays such as chalk-bearing stocks (est.: [22][25]).
- **Shape and scale:** craters 3-15 mm across and 1-5 mm deep (est.: no measured source; "shallow" [25]).
- **Signature per map:** a small pit with a white fleck that later turns body-coloured.
- **Sources:** [22][25][28].

### 4.8 Mortar recession and decay (sacrificial mortar)
- **Mechanism:** "the slow development of strength in weak lime mortars together with the scouring action of wind-borne grit and rain, and the spalling action of salt crystallisation and/or frost, gradually causes the exposed face of the mortar to disintegrate or 'weather' back" [15]. Mortar joints "are intended to be sacrificial" [17]. This applies whenever the mortar is the weaker, more permeable part, including cement-lime on dense modern brick (est.).
- **Acts on:** M1, then M2. **Removes:** mortar. **Reveals:** deeper mortar, with "the sand ... more exposed" [17], then bedding mortar.
- **Never:** widens the joint footprint; shows brick inside the footprint in plan (the recess floor is mortar, and the brick side walls are vertical); lowers the brick.
- **Where:** parapets, freestanding walls, gate piers, chimneys, building corners, below blocked or cracked downpipes, and under copings and sills with inadequate overhangs or missing drips [15]. Ivy roots and birds take loose lime too [15]. Wear varies per joint segment [B].
- **Shape and scale:** a concave recess following the joint; repoint is due when the mortar has weathered back about one joint width (≈10 mm) [18]. Differences between mortar and brick "only become apparent after many decades" when the mix is right [15]. Sand grains stand 0.5-2 mm proud of the binder (est.).
- **Progression:** N as-built profile. W 1-4 mm recession, binder gone, sand showing. H 5-15 mm, patchy, short empty sections, arrises exposed (then 4.1).
- **Interactions:** triggers 4.1; drives 4.16; ends in 4.9.
- **Signature per map:** height lower in joints, segment by segment, with no step at the joint centre-line and no step at T-junctions (*from the brick project*). Albedo trends to the sand colour [17] and darkens with depth and dirt (4.16). Roughness 0.93-0.97 [B]. AO deepens.
- **Sources:** [15][17][18][B].

### 4.9 Repointing: sympathetic and bad
- **Mechanism:** people rake out the outer 20-25 mm of mortar and refill it [17][18]. Good practice matches the sand, colour, texture and tooling, and recesses the new mortar slightly where the arrises are worn, to avoid "a joint which is visually wider than the actual joint" [17]. Bad practice uses hard grey cement with "featheredging along margins", or weather-struck, bucket-handle or ribbon (protruding) finishes in cement [16]. Ribbon pointing makes "the joints stand proud of the wall face" [15]. Power saws on thin joints break brick edges and overcut the head joints [17]. Mortar feathers spread onto the face if worked wet [18].
- **Acts on:** the mortar (replaced); in bad practice also the arris (disc damage removes brick; feathered mortar covers it). **Adds:** new mortar, which can sit above the envelope (ribbon, feathering). **Reveals:** brick body where discs cut the arris.
- **Never:** moves joint centrelines or the bond. Repointing is the only process allowed to change the visible mortar outline, and it needs its own mask.
- **Where:** in patches, because complete repointing of an elevation "is rarely needed or advisable" [18]. It follows the zones that decayed first (4.8 Where).
- **Shape and scale:** patch boundaries follow joint segments (est.). Feathered margins spread 2-10 mm onto the faces (est.). Ribbons stand 2-6 mm proud (est.). Disc-cut perpends are 2-6 mm wider than the bed joints, with straight sawn edges and nicks past the joint ends (est.: "overcutting on the head" joints [17]).
- **Progression:** fresh lime repoint: cleaner and lighter than old joints, blending over 5-20 years (est.). Cement repoint: stays grey, then drives 4.5.
- **Signature per map:** albedo: patch colour shifts and grey cement. Height: flush, proud or feathered. Roughness: cement smoother (est. 0.75-0.85). The `repoint` mask is excluded from the envelope check.
- **Sources:** [15][16][17][18].

### 4.10 Efflorescence
- **Mechanism:** a salt solution migrates to the surface and evaporates, leaving "a white, crystalline deposit" [33]. Most salts come from cement-based mortar, grout or concrete in contact with the brick, rarely from the brick itself [33]. ICOMOS: "whitish, powdery or whisker-like crystals", poorly bonded to the surface [28].
- **Acts on:** the surface. **Adds:** salt (a deposit). **Reveals:** nothing.
- **Never:** forms a uniform band of even width; appears far from a moisture path; changes the height noticeably (≤0.5 mm, est.).
- **Where:** where water leaves the wall: joints and the brick next to them, below copings, caps, sills and parapets, and at the base [33][32]. It is more visible on darker brick [32]. Bloom on a wall under a year old is "new building bloom" and fades; later efflorescence points to water penetration and is often worst in cold weather [33][32].
- **Shape and scale:** cloudy patches that are densest on the joint and fade 3-20 mm onto the brick face with a ragged edge (*from the brick project* [B]). The brick project's review targets: the face term peaks at about 1.10 x face luma, the joint term at ≥1.10 x the other joints, clustered on 10-18% of bricks (20-35 of 192) in the weathered preset, with no runs of 3+ along a course [B].
- **Progression:** N possible new-building bloom. W localised under sources. H extensive at the base and under sills, with subflorescence damage (4.4).
- **Signature per map:** albedo whitened toward Y 0.6-0.8 by partial coverage (est.). Roughness rises (powdery, est. 0.95). Height and normal about 0. Own `efflorescence` mask, also part of `deposit`.
- **Sources:** [28][32][33][B].

### 4.11 Lime run and calcite crusts
- **Mechanism:** a large quantity of water dissolves calcium compounds from cementitious materials; they react with atmospheric CO₂ to a "hard, crusty calcium carbonate deposit" that "will not wash away with rainwater". It continues until the water source is stopped [34].
- **Acts on:** the surface. **Adds:** a CaCO₃ crust (deposit). **Reveals:** nothing.
- **Never:** starts mid-brick without a crack or opening; runs upward.
- **Where:** from weep holes and fine cracks between brick and mortar [34]; under cement-bearing copings and trim [34][32].
- **Shape and scale:** "white or gray crusty formations" traced as the salt travels down the face [34]. Streaks are narrow at the source and widen and lobe downward, 0.1-1 m long (est.). Crust 0.5-3 mm thick (est.).
- **Signature per map:** albedo white-grey. Roughness lower than brick (glassy crust, est. 0.6-0.8). Height +0.5-3 mm inside a `deposit` mask. Strictly downward from its source.
- **Sources:** [32][34].

### 4.12 Soot, soiling and black crust (washed vs sheltered)
- **Mechanism:** atmospheric particles deposit and rain washes them off. Soiling patterns come from "dynamic competition" between deposition and rain washing [35]. Black crusts develop "on areas protected against direct rainfall or water runoff" in towns, from particles trapped in a gypsum matrix [28].
- **Acts on:** the surface. **Adds:** soiling, a very thin layer, or crust with visible thickness [28].
- **Never:** is darkest on rain-washed corners and top edges; follows ambient occlusion alone.
- **Where:** sheltered zones: under projections, in recesses and joints, the lower part of tall buildings. Clean zones: exposed corners, upper edges and roofs [35]. Wind-driven rain wets the windward top corners most, then the top and side edges, and the middle of large facades least [36].
- **Shape and scale:** soft-edged gradients set by shelter, with clean washed channels where runoff concentrates (est.). Within a tile, joints and porous or pitted faces hold more (est.). Industrial-era soiling was heavier: archival 1930s photos show much more than 1995, when the top third of the tower was "virtually free of soiling" [35].
- **Progression:** N none. W a grey film in sheltered zones. H black crust in sheltered zones and pale washed streaks.
- **Signature per map:** albedo darker and more neutral (multiply 0.3-0.8, est.). Height 0 for soiling, up to +1 mm for crust (est.). For a tiling mid-wall material, put the shelter field in a separate low-frequency mask or leave it to the engine.
- **Sources:** [28][35][36].

### 4.13 Runoff staining below sills, copings and projections
- **Mechanism:** water collects on horizontal features and runs down the face where a drip is missing or blocked. Drips and throats under sills "allow the correct management of rainwater to prevent excessive water running down the masonry" [38]. Copings need ≥40 mm projection and a drip [11]. Inadequate overhangs and missing drips speed up joint erosion below them [15]. Sills and copings are also salt sources [32].
- **Acts on:** the surface below the feature, plus 4.8 and 4.10 locally. **Adds:** dirt, salts or lime in streaks; it can also wash a streak cleaner than its surroundings (est.).
- **Never:** goes sideways or up; appears without a source above it.
- **Where:** below sill ends (where water concentrates), coping joints, window corners, downpipe leaks.
- **Shape and scale:** vertical streaks 50-300 mm wide and 0.3-2 m long, feathering out downward, often with darker edges and a cleaner centre (est.: no measured source).
- **Signature per map:** albedo streaks with their own mask. Height 0. Long streaks belong on a facade decal, not inside a small tile.
- **Sources:** [11][15][32][38].

### 4.14 Rising damp, tide marks and the splash zone
- **Mechanism:** capillary rise from the ground gives high moisture at the base, falling with height. A tide mark is "typically up to 1m above floor level, occasionally higher", and hygroscopic salts "concentrate in a band in the upper area of dampness" [38]. Rain splashes off hard paving onto the wall [40]. A DPC must sit ≥150 mm above ground [39], and the lowest 2 courses (150 mm) are engineering brick in severe exposure [11].
- **Acts on:** the lowest metre of brick and mortar. **Adds:** water, salts, splash dirt, algae. **Removes:** brick and mortar by salts (4.4).
- **Never:** appears high on the wall without a source; has a perfectly level, straight upper edge (est.).
- **Where:** splash band 0-0.3 m (est.: the 150 mm DPC rule [39] plus spread); damp and salt band up to about 1 m [38]; decay band 0.25-0.8 m [30].
- **Shape and scale:** a horizontal band with an undulating, ragged top (est.), darker below (a "moist area" [28]) with a white salt line at the top [38]. The splash band is speckled with soil colour and greening (est.).
- **Signature per map:** albedo darker below the line, white at the line, green-brown speckle at the foot; height loss in the decay band. Use a vertical-gradient mask; it does not belong in a mid-wall tile.
- **Sources:** [11][28][30][38][39][40].

### 4.15 Biological growth: algae, moss, lichen, ivy
- **Mechanism and look:** algae form powdery or viscous films 0.1 mm to several mm thick, green, red, brown or black, where the substrate "remains moistened for long periods", in areas exposed to rain or on water pathways; they do not adhere [28]. Mosses are "small, soft and green cushions of centimetric size" in cracks, cavities and wet places such as masonry joints, usually shady [28]. Lichens are rounded mm-cm crusty or bushy patches, grey, yellow, orange, green or black, best developed in clean air [28]. Ivy penetrates joints and feeds on the lime [15].
- **Drivers:** precipitation, humidity, thermal amplitude, distance from the sea and nearby vegetation; mineral substrates are colonised more often [37].
- **Acts on:** the surface; moss and ivy also loosen mortar. **Adds:** biomass (deposit). **Reveals:** nothing.
- **Never:** covers dry, rain-sheltered, sunny faces evenly; grows moss cushions on sound vertical faces away from joints or ledges (est.).
- **Where:** shaded and north- or east-facing walls (est.), the splash band, below leaks and sills, joints and ledges [28][37]. Lichens favour copings and wall tops (est.: exposed horizontal surfaces).
- **Shape and scale:** algae as streaky films following water paths. Moss as 10-50 mm cushions in joints (est., from "centimetric" [28]). Lichen as round colonies of a few mm to several cm [28].
- **Signature per map:** albedo green to black. Moss adds 2-15 mm of height inside joints (est.) and very high roughness. Algae films look smoother and darker when wet (est.). All in a `deposit` mask.
- **Sources:** [15][28][37].

### 4.16 Dirt in recessed joints and on ledges
- **Mechanism:** a raked joint "produces marked shadows and tends to darken the overall appearance of the wall" [1]. A recessed joint leaves a small shelf where water can collect [14], which is why recessed profiles are not recommended in severe exposure [11], are limited to 4 mm in the UK [10], and are not permitted in saline or polluted areas in Australia [8].
- **Acts on:** the joint surface and the underside of arrises. **Adds:** dirt (deposit).
- **Never:** forms a dark ring around fresh breaks (*from the brick project*); fills joints that are flush and rain-washed.
- **Where:** the bottom shelf of recessed bed joints, ledges of struck joints, rounded arrises, old spall floors.
- **Shape and scale:** graded with recess depth and shelter (est.); the shelf edge darkest.
- **Signature per map:** albedo 0.5-0.8 x in the recess (est.). Drive it by recess depth and an "up-facing" normal term, not by AO alone.
- **Sources:** [1][8][10][11][14].

### 4.17 Wetting and drying
- **Mechanism:** water filling the pores lowers the refractive contrast and enables "total internal reflection in the water film", so more light is absorbed and the surface darkens [44]. On weathered concrete, wetting cut *solar* reflectance by a mean of 0.23, ratios of about 0.4-0.6 per sample [43]; water absorbs strongly in the near infrared, so solar darkening overstates visible darkening. No measured visible wet/dry ratio for brick was found. ICOMOS calls the result a moist area: darkening due to dampness, shaped by its origin (pipe leakage, rising damp, hygroscopic salts, condensation) [28].
- **Acts on:** both components. **Adds:** water (no height change).
- **Never:** brightens; makes the whole wall glossy without darkening it.
- **Where:** the wind-driven-rain pattern: windward top corners and edges first [36]. Joints and the base dry last (est.: higher porosity and capillary supply [38]). Bricks of different absorption dry at different rates, so drying walls show a per-brick patchwork (est.).
- **Shape and scale:** darkening is multiplicative per brick and joint; drying fronts are ragged.
- **Signature per map:** albedo x 0.55-0.75 (est.: milder than the solar ratios of [43], for the reason above). Roughness stays high on porous brick once the water has soaked in; only running or standing films are glossy (0.1-0.3, est.); dense engineering and flashed bricks hold films longest (est.).
- **Sources:** [28][36][38][43][44].

### 4.18 Sulfate attack of cement mortar
- **Mechanism:** sulfate in solution (from S0/S1 bricks, ground water or flue gases) reacts with the tricalcium aluminate of Portland cement to form ettringite, which expands. "Cracking of the bed joint mortar is characteristic of sulfate attack", followed by "crumbling and erosion of the mortar joint" and, in severe cases, expansion and bowing of the wall [46]. It is uncommon, slow (several years), and needs long saturation and water moving through the wall [46].
- **Acts on:** cement-bearing mortar (M1, M2). **Removes:** mortar, after cracking it. Expansion can push crumbled mortar slightly proud (est.); that is the one aging process that adds height on the mortar without a deposit, so give it a mask and exclude it from the envelope check.
- **Never:** occurs in non-hydraulic lime mortar, which has no Portland cement and so no C3A [46]; moves the joint footprint (bowing of the whole wall is out of scope); starts on dry, sheltered walls.
- **Where:** parapets and free-standing walls without copings, chimneys, retaining walls, below sills without DPCs, and the courses near ground [46].
- **Shape and scale:** a hairline crack along the middle of bed joints first, then whitish, crumbling joints; perpends less affected (est.: bed joints carry the load and the water).
- **Signature per map:** height: a thin dark crack line along bed-joint centres, then patchy loss like 4.8. Albedo: lighter, whiter crumbled mortar (est.). Mask: `sulfate`.
- **Sources:** [46].

### 4.19 Vanadium and manganese stains
- **Vanadium:** yellow, green or purple salt deposits "in the heart of light-colored brick units", from vanadium salts in the clay; most conspicuous on light bricks, and worsened by acid cleaning [34]. They sit mid-face, the opposite of joint-centred efflorescence, so buff and yellow stocks are the susceptible kind.
- **Manganese:** brown stains on the mortar joints that can extend onto the brick faces, from tan, brown, black or grey bricks coloured with manganese and cleaned with acid [34].
- **Acts on:** the surface. **Adds:** a thin stain (no height). Own `deposit` mask. Never on every brick; vanadium never starts in the joint.
- **Sources:** [34].

## 5. Invariants

Regions are defined in section 9 (JSON block) and built from layout masks or process masks, never from the height they constrain. In short: `mortar` = mask_mortar ≥ 0.5; `mortar_core` = mask_mortar ≥ 0.99 (excludes the anti-aliased ramp, which sits at brick height); `unit` = mask_mortar < 0.01, minus `repoint` when the variant has one; `damage_raw` = mask_damage ≥ 0.5; `damage` = damage_raw within `unit`; `face` = unit, not damage, eroded 3 mm; `arris_edge` = the 1.5 mm unit band along the joint, not damaged; `arris_band` = unit within 12 mm of mortar. `nowear` is the same preset rendered with every wear, damage and deposit parameter at 0. Two preset switches recur below: `joint_profile` (flush, struck, concave, weatherstruck, raked/recessed) and `mortar_vs_brick` (sacrificial, equal, mortar_harder).

**I1. The joint footprint is fixed by the layout.**
- **Statement:** mask_mortar, the joint centrelines and the bond are identical in every variant and at every wear level.
- **Why:** mortar sits beside the brick face, not beneath it. Aging removes material in depth, never sideways across the brick/mortar boundary [15][16][17].
- **Enforce:** mask_mortar comes only from the layout generator (tile generator plus arris threshold), never from the final height. Damage, erosion and deposits read it and never write it. Repointing shows its own `repoint` mask on top.
- **Check:** `layout_fixed` (mask_invariance against nowear) hard; `joint_median` (run_length with `max_mm: 35`, so bed-joint runs along a whole course and bed + perp + bed runs are dropped) hard; `mortar_fraction` soft. Measure joint width on mask_mortar only: a joint measured on height (`height_split`) widens with arris wear (I7), so use it only in N.

**I2. Removal only goes down.**
- **Statement:** erosion, spalls, chips, lime pops, mortar recession and disc damage never rise above the as-built envelope. Only deposits, sulfate expansion (4.18) and repointing may.
- **Why:** they remove material (section 3).
- **Enforce:** each component's height = as-built minus removal; guards may only clamp a removal, never lift a surface above its as-built level; deposits are combined afterwards inside their masks; repointing after that inside `repoint`.
- **Check:** `envelope` hard, with `except: [deposit, repoint]` (plus `sulfate` when used).

**I3. Brick loss reveals brick.**
- **Statement:** inside a unit footprint, every exposed layer belongs to that brick: its surface or its body. Damage colour comes from the brick's own colour, never from the mortar colour, and damage never claims joint pixels.
- **Why:** B2 lies beneath B1 in the same unit [20][21][25][27].
- **Enforce:** body colour = a small lift or tint of the brick's own per-ID colour [B]; mortar colour only inside mask_mortar; export as mask_damage the mask that actually drives the damage height and colour, so damage that leaks into the joint shows in it.
- **Check:** `no_damage_in_joint` (coverage of `damage_raw` within `mortar_core` ≤ 0.0005) hard; against `mortar` ≥ 0.5 the ramp pixels give 0.1-0.9% on correct maps [B], hence the core. `damage_identity` (per_element corr of damage luma with face luma per brick ≥ 0.5, est.; [B] measured 0.55-0.91) and `damage_lift` (boundary_profile of basecolor inside damage divided by the local face level, 1.00-1.15; [B] measured 1.09-1.14) soft. A per-brick ratio of damage to its own face luma has no check stat yet; damage_lift uses a 20 mm local face mean instead.

**I4. Mortar loss reveals mortar.**
- **Statement:** inside the joint footprint the recess floor is mortar (sand-rich), never brick-coloured.
- **Enforce:** mortar albedo is selected by mask_mortar only; recession only lowers mortar height.
- **Check:** I1, plus `albedo_mortar` inside the section 7 mortar band.

**I5. Damaged brick and the local mortar level.** The rule depends on the joint profile and on which material is weaker.
- **Statement, raked and recessed joints (any `mortar_vs_brick`):** no damaged brick pixel drops to the local mortar level. This is the user's acceptance criterion from the brick project (raked joints 5.8-7.4 mm), which built a guard of local mortar + 1 mm [B]. It caps chip and spall depth at about the recess minus 1 mm: 5-6 mm on a 6-7 mm rake, which holds the brick project's measured medians (2.4-3.2 mm [B]) and most of the W range of section 9. A deeper break would expose the mortar's side face as in (d) of section 3; in a raked-joint material that is a deliberate deviation and needs this check switched off.
- **Statement, flush, struck, concave and weatherstruck joints:** chips, spalls and lime pops are per-brick events and may drop below the mortar face. The mortar then stands round the hollow as a ledge: "the surrounding mortar can remain rigid and protrude around the edges of the hollowed brick" [31]. Its side face shows, but the joint keeps its width in plan, so I1 and I3 still hold. A spall never lowers or widens the mortar next to it.
- **Statement, mortar_vs_brick.** *Sacrificial* (mortar weaker and more permeable: lime on any brick, and cement-lime on dense modern brick; the default): the mortar recedes before the arrises round [15][17], so arris wear and face erosion stay at or above the local joint face. *Equal* (both recede together, the ideal of [15]): no ordering beyond I1 and I2. *Mortar_harder* (cement repointing or strong historic mixes on soft or under-fired brick [15][16]): the brick decays beside the joint and the mortar stands proud of the arris band (4.5).
- **Enforce:** raked/recessed: `brick_h0 = max(face − all removal, mortar_local + 1 mm)`, applied to damage too [B]. Flush and shallow profiles: guard only arris wear and face erosion, `min(face, max(face − wear, mortar_local))`, and leave damage unguarded; recession never reads the damage mask. Mortar_harder: lower `brick_h0` inside `arris_band` and keep the mortar at its pointed level.
- **Check:** raked/recessed: `order_raked` (damage vs `mortar_core` p95, margin 0) hard, measured 0 violations on the brick project [B]; `guard_1mm` (margin 1 mm) soft. Flush and shallow: `no_damage_in_joint` and `layout_fixed` (hard, above) carry the footprint rule, plus `mortar_by_damage` (mortar next to damage no lower than mortar elsewhere) soft. Sacrificial W/H on flush profiles: `arris_over_mortar` soft (est.). Mortar_harder: `proud_cement` soft.

**I6. Each defect belongs to one brick.**
- **Statement:** chips, spalls, lime pops and per-brick tone sit on one unit. They never straddle or mirror across a joint (*from the brick project*).
- **Enforce:** seed chips and spalls per brick ID, with half-brick masks per arris side, and mask by the owner brick before combining [B].
- **Check:** straddling shows as damage in the joint: `no_damage_in_joint` (I3). Mirroring across a joint (both bricks chipped at the same spot) has no check type; the brick project measured it as the co-location probability of chips across bed joints against a control offset [B]. Inspect the `masks` crops from `previews.py`.

**I7. Rounded arrises stay brick.**
- **Statement:** arris rounding changes height inside the unit band, not the mask. The opening between the faces widens (section 4.1); the footprint does not.
- **Enforce:** the arris profile comes from the layout distance field inside `unit`; the mask threshold is not recomputed from height.
- **Check:** raked/recessed (a step ≥3.6 mm): `arris_rise` and `arris_peak` (edge_profile) and `arris_aa`. Flush and shallow: `arris_drop` (height of `arris_edge` minus face). All soft and est. except the N values measured on the brick project [B]. A band slope (`slope` between unit and mortar) is not used: it pools the flat face and joint into the band, read 33-44° on the crisp brick project maps (78-80° peak), and can rise as the edge rounds.

**I8. Depths are bounded by the unit and the joint construction.**
- **Statement:** spalls stay inside the unit (typically 2-12 mm, ≤25 mm; est.); new chips follow the ASTM class sizes [20]; joint recess follows the profile and region (≤4 mm UK raked [10], ≤10 mm AU [8]); recession reaches bedding mortar only after 20-25 mm [17][18].
- **Check:** `spall_depth` (damage minus face) and `recess` (face minus mortar) in section 9.

**I9. Deposits obey their sources and gravity.**
- **Statement:** efflorescence starts at joints and water exits; lime run and runoff streaks only run downward from a source; soot sits where rain does not wash [33][34][35][28]. Vanadium stains are the exception that sits mid-face (4.19).
- **Enforce:** build deposit masks from sources (joint mask, crack and weep points, sill lines, a shelter field) with downward-only directional blur or warp; keep each deposit in its own mask (efflorescence as `mask_efflorescence`).
- **Check:** `efflor_origin`: `coverage {region: mortar_d5, within: deposit_eff}`, the share of efflorescence pixels within 5 mm of a joint, ≥ 0.6 (est.: the brick project's joint-centred target [B]), soft.

**I10. Colour is per unit, and the tile shows no lattice.**
- **Check:** `brick_cv`, `brick_band` (`band` is a single float, 0.14 = ±14%: the brick project's tiling target, not a real-wall limit [B]), `lowfreq_40`, `lowfreq_300` and `seam`. All soft.

**I11. Material response keeps its order.**
- **Statement:** roughness rises from the fired face to the broken body to the mortar [B]; cement pointing is smoother than lime (est.).
- **Check:** `rough_order` soft.

**I12. Wetting only darkens.**
- **Check (wet variant, soft, est.):** `value_range {map: basecolor, region: unit, space: linear, stat: p50}` within 0.55-0.75 x the dry render's value (section 4.17). `coverage {region: rough_low, within: not_film}` ≤ 0.001, with `rough_low` = roughness ≤ 0.4 and `not_film` = everything but the `film` mask.

A whole missing brick would change the layout. It is out of scope; if added, it needs its own mask and is exempt from I1.

## 6. Common procedural mistakes

| Wrong (what procedural materials often do) | Right (physics) | How to fix it in the graph |
|---|---|---|
| Erosion grows the mortar mask, so worn walls show wider grey joints | brick loss reveals brick; the joint keeps its footprint [17] | mortar mask from the layout only; erosion lowers brick height inside `unit` |
| Damage reveals mortar colour or a generic "under-brick" grey | the body of the same brick, near its face hue, or a yellow or grey core [21][20] | body colour derived from each brick's own colour; small lift; Δb* within ±1.5 [B] |
| Fresh spall floors drawn as saturated cream decals | depth and rake carry the break; the floor is the same brick's body, a little lighter when fresh [21][20] | small lift and tint (the brick project's target: 1.04-1.15 x face luma [B]); old breaks keep the face's grime [B] |
| Dark ring of dirt around every break (AO fed into albedo) | a fresh break exposes clean body [20][21]; an old one weathers like the face around it (est.) | guard AO-dirt off damage edges; use a flat-face AO value on floors (*from the brick project* [B]) |
| Uniform soft bevel on every new brick | crisp arrises: ≤1 mm wirecut, 1-3 mm stock (est.); rounding comes with age (4.1) | 1 px anti-aliased edge; rounding driven by wear and exposure |
| Mortar and brick erode together at random, or the order keyed to the binder's name | the weaker, more permeable material recedes first: usually the mortar [15]; mortar harder than the brick leaves it proud [16]; equal wear is a third case [15] | one `mortar_vs_brick` switch: sacrificial, equal, mortar_harder (I5) |
| Damage guarded above the mortar on every profile, so a flush wall's spalls float above the face | in flush and shallow joints a deep spall drops below the mortar face and leaves a ledge [31]; the joint keeps its width | guard damage only for raked/recessed joints (the brick project's rule [B]); never let a guard lift a surface above the envelope (I2, I5) |
| Spall in a flush wall that lowers or widens the neighbouring joint | the mortar beside a spall keeps its height and footprint [31] | recession never reads the damage mask; check `mortar_by_damage` |
| Joints all deeply raked by default | UK recessed ≤4 mm [10]; most walls bucket-handle, weatherstruck or flush [10][11]; AU raked ≤10 mm [8] | profile and depth from the region and profile inputs |
| Mortar wear as a uniform groove, or with a cliff along the joint centre-line | joints weather back patchily, worst where they stay wet [15]; a joint face has no step along its centre (est.) | per-segment IDs; smooth within each segment and across T-junctions (*from the brick project* [B]) |
| Continuous noise for brick colour, crossing joints | each brick has its own firing; variation is per unit, plus within-face mottling [19][21] | flood-fill IDs to random colour; mottling inside each brick |
| Hero bricks far outside the population | per-brick tone comes from firing and blending [19][12]; on a tile, distinctive bricks repeat at the period and print a lattice | clamp per-brick tone (the brick project's tiling target 0.86-1.14 x mean [B]); decorrelate randoms; expose brick_id |
| Spalls placed on the darkest, flashed ends | within one clay, hotter-fired (darker) brick is denser and stronger [19]; pale, soft bricks spall [21] | bias damage toward light, low-density bricks; never share a random with the flash |
| Chips mirrored across joints, or rectangular shelves | chips break pieces off the edges of one brick [28][20], conchoidal on hard bricks [29] | per-side samplers masked by the owner brick; taper into the face (*from the brick project* [B]) |
| Flat-tray pores 30+ mm across ("thumbprints") | fired faces carry pinholes and small cupped voids, with smoother fire-skin patches between (est.) | remove blobs > 10 mm; cup-shaped depth (*from the brick project* [B]) |
| Efflorescence as a white underline under every brick | joint-centred, ragged, clustered, under water sources [33][32] | gate on joints and sources; no plateau after a threshold [B] |
| Moss and lichen sprinkled evenly on faces | moss in joints, cracks and ledges in damp shade; lichen as round colonies on tops [28] | masks from joints, recess depth, moisture and orientation |
| Soot from AO alone | sheltered areas soiled, rain-washed corners and edges clean [35][36] | separate shelter field; joints and recesses get more |
| Runoff streaks with no source or going sideways | streaks hang below sills, copings, weeps and cracks [34][38] | seed from source lines; downward-only directional warp |
| Perfectly uniform joints in handmade work, or random perpend wander | joints absorb unit range; perpends stay aligned every other course [1][3][4] | jitter within the R category; snap perpends to the bond |
| Wet = add gloss everywhere | wet = darker (x 0.55-0.75, est.) [44]; gloss only where a film exists (est.) | multiply albedo; lower roughness only in a film mask |
| Kiln flash as random gradients on every brick | flash concentrates on ends or faces that met the fire or gas; Flemish headers flare [19][21][26] | one flash end per selected brick; header-specific flash for Flemish |
| Perforations visible on stretcher faces | holes are in the bed faces; ≥19 mm from the face [20] | show cores only on cut bricks or very deep breaks |

## 8. Variants and presets

**Variant ladder** (process numbers from section 4):
- **New (N):** as-built envelope; crisp arrises (4.1 at as-made radius); chips at the ASTM class rates [20]; optional new-building bloom (4.10); colour blended from packs [12]; no soiling.
- **Weathered (W):** 4.1 at 2-4 mm; 4.2 light; 4.3 on 2-6% of brick area; 4.6 in-service chips; 4.8 recession of 1-4 mm with sand showing; 4.10 localised; 4.12 grey film in shelter and joints; 4.15 algae in damp zones; 4.16 joint dirt. The brick project's weathered preset is close to this (4.3% damage, 7.3 mm raked joints) [B], but keeps crisp arrises by the user's choice.
- **Heavily weathered (H):** 4.1 at 5-10 mm; 4.2 differential recession between bricks; 4.3 on 10-30% of bricks in exposed zones; 4.8 recession of 5-15 mm with empty sections; moss in joints; black crust in shelter; lichen on tops. Use 4.4 and 4.14 only if the tile includes the base of the wall.
- **Cement-repointed (opt-in):** W or H bricks plus bad 4.9 (grey, feathered, widened perps, shrinkage hairlines) and 4.5 dishing of 2-5 mm (W) or 5-15 mm (H); `mortar_vs_brick` = mortar_harder, and `unit` excludes the `repoint` mask so feathered cement does not count as brick. Optionally 4.18 in wet locations.
- **Equal wear (opt-in):** a well-matched historic wall: brick and mortar recede together, joints barely recessed after decades [15]; `mortar_vs_brick` = equal.
- **Lime-repointed (opt-in):** H bricks with fresh lime joints recessed 1-3 mm, as patches lighter than the old joints.
- **Wet (modifier):** any variant plus 4.17.
- **Unblended (opt-in):** banded packs (section 1.4), for poor-workmanship scenes.

**Interview questions** (recommended default in brackets):
1. Region and unit size? [UK 215 x 102.5 x 65 mm, 10 mm joints]
2. Bond? [stretcher, half lap; Flemish or English for UK solid walls before about 1930 (est.)]
3. Forming type: wirecut, machine stock, handmade, pressed? [machine stock for heritage, textured wirecut for modern]
4. Colour family and blend: red, yellow/buff, brown multi, blue, a blend? [red multi; yellow stock for London-type heritage]
5. Mortar binder and colour, and is the mortar weaker than the brick? [lime, off-white Y about 0.5, for heritage; grey cement-lime for modern; `mortar_vs_brick` sacrificial in both cases, mortar_harder only for cement repointing or strong mixes on soft brick]
6. Joint profile? [bucket handle for modern UK; flush, weathered lime for heritage; recessed ≤4 mm UK or ≤10 mm AU only on request]. Say what follows: in a raked joint the damage stays above the recessed mortar (spall depth ≤ recess − 1 mm); in a flush joint a deep spall goes below the mortar face and leaves it as a ledge.
7. Which age variants? [N, W, H as three wrappers over one core graph]
8. Repointing state? [none; offer cement-repointed as an extra variant]
9. Exposure and climate: frost, coastal salt, urban soot, damp shade? [temperate urban, moderate frost]
10. Tile role: mid-wall field, or a full-height strip with base zone and coping? [mid-wall field; base, sill and coping effects as separate masks or decals]
11. Viewing distance? [1.8 m tile at 2048, 0.88 mm/px; 0.9 m for close-ups]
12. Extra outputs? [mask_mortar, mask_damage (the mask that drives damage height and colour), mask_deposit, mask_efflorescence, mask_repoint, brick_id]
13. Wet variant? [no; but output a per-brick absorbency mask so the engine can wet the wall]

## 10. Substance Designer build notes

**Layout generator.** Tile Generator, Square pattern, Offset 0.5 for a half lap. Scale = brick / pitch, derived from §2:

| Layout | Tile | X x Y | Scale X | Scale Y |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| UK stretcher | 1.8 m | 8 x 24 | 0.9556 (215/225) | 0.8667 (65/75) [B] |
| US modular stretcher | 1.626 m | 8 x 24 | 0.9531 | 0.8437 |
| AU stretcher | 1.2 x 1.204 m | 5 x 14 | 0.9583 | 0.8837 |
| DE NF stretcher | 1.5 m | 6 x 18 | 0.9600 | 0.8520 |

- **Flemish:** two Tile Generators per tile, each X = pairs, Y = courses, Offset 0.5 (course B shifts by 168.75 mm = half a 337.5 mm pair). Stretchers use scale X 0.637 (215/337.5). Headers use scale X 0.304 (102.5/337.5), shifted 225/337.5 = 0.667 of a pair. Combine with Max.
- **English bond:** a stretcher generator on even courses (Y = courses/2, scale Y 65/150) and a header generator on odd courses (X = 2 x bricks, scale X 102.5/112.5, half-header offset), shifted one course vertically. Combine with Max.
- **Common bond:** stretcher courses minus every 6th course, plus a header-course generator with Y = courses/6 (or /12 when the stretcher offsets resume after the header course).
- The Brick Generator node is faster for plain running bond but cannot place Flemish headers.
- **Irregularity:** one low-amplitude, low-frequency warp of the layout, scaled by the unit range category (R2 tight, R1 loose [3]) and applied upstream of everything, so all masks agree. Compensate the mean size so the median joint stays on spec [B].

**Per-unit IDs and randoms.** Flood Fill, then Flood Fill to Random Grayscale and Random Color for tone; Flood Fill to Gradient for one-ended effects (flash end, spall growth side), each with its **own** seed; Flood Fill to BBox Size to classify headers (102.5 mm wide) from stretchers (215 mm), for flared headers in Flemish bond [26]. Expose `brick_id` so engines can add a world-space per-brick tint that breaks the tile repeat [B].

**Height composition order** (from the brick project, raked joints [B]; the switches of I5 noted):
1. `face` = per-brick level + tilt + undulation + pores, with tilt and undulation inside the 1.6-2.4 mm distortion limits [20], plus lipping between neighbours (section 2, est.).
2. Damage (chips, spalls, pops) is computed **without** the unit mask, normalised-blurred (blur(D x unit) / blur(unit)), given asymmetric Levels for the rake wall, and only then hard-masked by `unit`. Masking first leaves fins. Export this final mask as mask_damage.
3. `brick_h0` = face − arris wear − face erosion − damage depth, then guarded by `joint_profile` (I5):
   - raked or recessed: `max(brick_h0, mortar_local + 1 mm)` over all removal, damage included [B];
   - flush, struck, concave, weatherstruck: guard only the wear terms, `min(face, max(face − wear, mortar_local))` when sacrificial, and leave damage unguarded so a deep spall can drop below the joint face [31].
   A guard only clamps a removal; it never lifts a pixel above `face` (I2).
4. `mortar_h` = profile depth + sand grain + per-segment wear. Recession never reads mask_damage.
5. `brick_h` = lerp(mortar_h, brick_h0, arris), where the arris ramp comes from the layout. This keeps a 1-px anti-aliased step with no bevel.
6. `height` = brick_h. The brick project took max(brick_h, mortar_h) here, which lifts any brick pixel below the mortar plane: that is the raked guard in another form, so keep it only for raked or recessed joints. For mortar_harder, lower `brick_h0` inside `arris_band` and keep the mortar at its pointed level, so the mortar stands proud.
7. Deposits (crust, moss, lime run, sulfate expansion) are added inside their masks; repointing and ribbons last, inside `repoint`. `unit` for every check excludes `repoint`, so feathered cement never counts as brick face.

**Masks that come from the layout only:** mask_mortar (from the arris threshold, never from final height), brick_id, joint segment IDs (shifted Tile Generator IDs for bed and perp joints [B]), course index, header/stretcher class.

**Colour chain:** per-brick colour from IDs, then within-face mottling, flash on one end of selected bricks, specks and inclusions. Break colour comes from the brick's own colour (small HSL lift, slight tint) [B]. Mortar colour goes in by mask. Deposits follow in order: shelter soiling, recess-driven joint dirt, joint-gated efflorescence, biology, then lime run downward from its sources. Keep AO-based dirt off fresh breaks [B].

**Roughness chain:** face < break < mortar [B]; each deposit has its own value.

**Brick-specific pitfalls** (*from the brick project* [B]):
- Flood Fill randoms are 0 on mortar pixels. To give joints per-brick or per-segment values (efflorescence gate, wear), propagate the IDs into the joints with Distance (source = per-brick random, combine distance off).
- Shared randoms couple features (flash and spall at the same end; absorbency and grime on the same bricks) and print a lattice. Give each feature its own seed, and bias spalls toward pale, soft bricks, not flashed ends [19].
- Histogram Scan centres its threshold at 1 - Position. Re-calibrate thresholds from measured quantiles after any upstream change, against the coverage targets of §9.
- Warp is strong (0.05 at 2048 ≈ 2.4 px std); use smooth Perlin gradients for the layout warp.
- Small floating spall fragments (under about 15 mm, detached from a larger spall) read as noise; remove them with an opening on the spall mask. No check type separates them from legitimate chips.
- Use Height to Normal World Units with the true tile size and height depth (DirectX), HBAO in world units, and 16-bit height chains. Engine stalls and fine-noise choices are in `sd_craft.md`.
